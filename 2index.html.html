Introduction / Statement of Purpose

SECTION 1 — CONTENT PORTION · What to write: 3–4 pages
Open with impact: "On 14 September 2019, two drone strikes on Saudi Arabia's Abqaiq oil facility caused the single largest one-day oil price jump in history — a 15% spike that reverberated through every petrol pump in India within weeks. The source of those drones traced back to tensions involving Iran, a country over 2,000 km from India." Then explain: (1) Global oil market structure — OPEC produces 40% of world oil. Iran is the 3rd largest OPEC producer. When Iran is under sanctions or conflict, global supply tightens immediately. (2) Transmission mechanism — oil price rise → transport costs → food inflation → general CPI rises. (3) India's specific vulnerability — India imports 85% of crude oil. Every $10/barrel rise = $15 billion extra in import bill annually. Close with: "This project analyses the causal chain from Iran conflict escalations to global crude oil disruptions to the inflation felt by ordinary households in Betul, Madhya Pradesh, using secondary data from international financial institutions and primary survey data."

Need of the Study

1. INDIA'S ENERGY VULNERABILITY: India imports 85% of crude oil — any Iran conflict directly threatens macroeconomic stability. 2. STRAIT OF HORMUZ RISK: 20% of world oil passes through the Strait of Hormuz, which Iran has repeatedly threatened to close. 3. HOUSEHOLD-LEVEL IMPACT: Oil price shocks are felt through petrol/diesel hikes, LPG price increases, and rising food prices. 4. MONETARY POLICY: Rising oil prices force RBI to raise repo rate — increasing EMIs for millions of Indian households. 5. POLICY RELEVANCE: India's energy transition, strategic petroleum reserve building, and rupee internationalisation are directly shaped by Iran-related oil shocks.
◎

Objective of the Study

1. To trace the relationship between Iran conflict escalations and Brent crude oil price movements from January 2018 to April 2024. 2. To analyse how global oil price increases transmit into Consumer Price Index (CPI) inflation in India, the USA, and the European Union. 3. To assess the level of public awareness regarding the Iran conflict-inflation connection through a primary survey conducted in Betul, Madhya Pradesh. 4. To understand India's specific macroeconomic vulnerability from its high crude oil import dependence using secondary data from IMF, World Bank and RBI. 5. To suggest short-term and long-term policy measures for reducing India's exposure to geopolitical oil price shocks.

Review of Literature

Collect research papers related to your topic, read and explain in brief with the name of the Researcher Quoted — official guideline requirement.
PAPER 1 — Baumeister & Peersman (2013)
AUTHORS: Baumeister, C. and Peersman, G. · TITLE: 'Time-Varying Effects of Oil Supply Shocks on the US Economy' · JOURNAL: Macroeconomic Dynamics, Cambridge University Press, 2013. KEY FINDING: Oil supply reductions cause proportionally larger price spikes than equivalent supply increases reduce prices — the asymmetric price response. This asymmetry is driven by market psychology and precautionary stockpiling. RELEVANCE: Directly explains why Iran sanctions (which reduced Iran's exports from 2.5M to 0.3M barrels/day) caused disproportionately large global price spikes — the asymmetric response amplifies the impact of supply shocks from Iran.
PAPER 2 — Kilian L. (2008)
AUTHOR: Kilian, Lutz · TITLE: 'The Economic Effects of Energy Price Shocks' · JOURNAL: Journal of Economic Literature, American Economic Association, 2008. KEY FINDING: Kilian distinguishes precautionary demand shocks — markets buy oil in advance due to fear of future supply disruption, driving prices up before any actual supply loss. RELEVANCE: The April 2024 Iran-Israel direct military confrontation caused Brent crude to spike even though no actual oil supply was disrupted. Pure precautionary demand. The mere fear of Iran blocking the Strait of Hormuz drove prices up — the geopolitical risk premium.
PAPER 3 — IMF World Economic Outlook (2022)
AUTHOR: International Monetary Fund · TITLE: 'Commodity Market Disruptions, Growth and Inflation' · SOURCE: IMF World Economic Outlook, April 2022, Chapter 3. KEY FINDING: A sustained $10/barrel increase in oil prices raises global inflation by 0.5 percentage points and reduces global GDP growth by 0.2 percentage points. For oil-importing developing economies like India, the impact is significantly larger. RELEVANCE: Provides the mathematical framework for estimating India's inflation response. When Brent averaged $99/barrel in 2022 (vs $71 in 2021, a $28 rise), this predicts ~1.4pp of additional inflation — which closely matches India's actual CPI increase from 5.1% to 6.7%.
PAPER 4 — RBI Annual Report (2023)
AUTHOR: Reserve Bank of India · TITLE: Annual Report 2022–23, External Sector Chapter · SOURCE: Reserve Bank of India, www.rbi.org.in, 2023. KEY FINDING: India's Current Account Deficit widened to 2.7% of GDP in 2022–23 driven by ₹1.7 lakh crore increase in petroleum import bill. Every $10/barrel Brent crude increase adds 30–35 basis points to India's headline CPI inflation. RELEVANCE: Most authoritative India-specific source for the oil-inflation quantification. The RBI's 30–35 bps figure provides the quantitative backbone for the Analysis & Findings section of this project.

Short & Long Term Implications of the Topic

SHORT TERM (0–2 years)
1. Direct fuel price hike → petrol/diesel retail prices rise → every transport cost increases. 2. Food inflation: truckers pass fuel surcharges within 2–3 weeks → food CPI rises sharply. 3. Rupee depreciation: higher oil import bill → more dollar demand → rupee weakens → all imports costlier. 4. RBI rate hikes: to contain inflation → repo rate rises → EMIs on home/vehicle/business loans increase → economic slowdown risk. 5. Corporate cost pressure: airlines, logistics, manufacturing face rising energy costs → profit margins compress.
LONG TERM (2–10 years)
1. Energy transition acceleration: every Iran conflict event strengthens the case for solar, wind, green hydrogen. India targets 500 GW renewable by 2030. 2. Strategic Petroleum Reserve expansion: India building to 90-day buffer (currently ~67 days) at Vizag, Mangalore, Padur. 3. OPEC+ power shift: Iran suppression forces Saudi Arabia/UAE to fill gap → their leverage in global oil pricing increases. 4. Rupee internationalisation: India paying for oil in rupees (UAE, Russia deals) — reduces dollar-dependency. 5. Oil source diversification: India importing from Russia (discounted crude post-Ukraine) and USA (shale oil) to reduce Middle East dependence.

Research Question / Hypothesis

RESEARCH QUESTION: "Does the geopolitical conflict involving Iran cause measurable increases in global crude oil prices, and do these price increases transmit into elevated CPI inflation in India and other oil-importing economies?" HYPOTHESIS: "The geopolitical conflict involving Iran from 2018 to 2024 has caused repeated, measurable spikes in global crude oil prices (Brent crude), which have subsequently transmitted into elevated CPI inflation in India and globally, with the magnitude of impact being significantly greater for import-dependent economies like India." NULL HYPOTHESIS (H₀): "There is no significant relationship between Iran conflict escalations and movements in global crude oil prices during 2018–2024." ALTERNATE HYPOTHESIS (H₁): "There is a significant positive relationship between Iran conflict escalations and Brent crude oil price increases during 2018–2024, which then transmit into higher CPI inflation in oil-importing economies." This project aims to ACCEPT the Alternate Hypothesis (H₁) based on secondary data analysis and primary survey findings.

Challenges Encountered while preparing the Project

This is a COMPULSORY section per official guidelines. Be honest and specific. Do NOT skip it.
1. ISOLATING IRAN'S CONTRIBUTION: The most difficult challenge was separating Iran's specific impact from simultaneous factors: COVID-19 demand crash (2020), Russia-Ukraine war energy disruption (2022), and US Federal Reserve interest rate hikes (which strengthened the dollar and suppressed oil demand). These events overlapped with the Iran conflict period, making pure causal attribution difficult. 2. DATA CROSS-REFERENCING: Finding accurate historical Brent crude data by specific Iran events required cross-referencing World Bank Commodity Price Data, US EIA (Energy Information Administration) and OPEC Statistical Bulletin — since no single source had all required data in one place. 3. SURVEY LIMITATIONS: Collecting 30+ genuine responses from Betul within limited time was challenging. Some respondents were unfamiliar with terms like "Brent crude" and "Current Account Deficit," requiring simplified question language that may have reduced precision. 4. CENTRAL TENDENCY FORMULAS: Applying the Median formula for grouped data (using cumulative frequency) required multiple practice attempts. Identifying the correct "median class" from the cumulative frequency table was initially confusing. 5. TIME MANAGEMENT: Managing this project alongside the BST project (both due June 10) and summer vacation required disciplined scheduling — Economics limited to 1.5–2 hour evening sessions while BST received morning priority.

Calculate MEAN from grouped frequency table — full working shown (1 page)
Method: Direct — Mean = Σ(f × m) ÷ Σf where m = midpoint of each class interval. Steps: (1) Write grouped frequency table. (2) Find midpoint of each class. (3) Calculate f × m for each row. (4) Sum all f × m values. (5) Divide by total frequency n=30. Write interpretation after answer: 'The mean rating of X.X indicates that on average, respondents feel a moderate-to-significant impact of oil-driven inflation on daily life, confirming the macro-to-micro transmission of Iran conflict effects...'
Calculate MEDIAN and MODE from same grouped data — full working (1 page)
MEDIAN: (1) Build cumulative frequency column. (2) Find median class where cf first exceeds n/2 = 15. (3) Formula: Median = L + [(n/2 - cf) / f] × h. Show ALL substitution and working. MODE: (1) Identify modal class (highest frequency). (2) Formula: Mode = L + [(f1–f0)/((f1–f0)+(f1–f2))] × h. Show working and interpretation.
